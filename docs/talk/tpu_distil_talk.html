TPU-Distil · October 2026

Big-model quality
at a small-model price

How we taught small models to do the job of a 552B model on SQL, finance and maths, and served each one on a single Cloud TPU chip
Jawad Amin and Fred
github.com/jawadaminGOOG/tpu-distil
Welcome. This talk is about getting the quality of a big model at the price of a small one. A frontier model does specialist work well, but it needs a whole 8-GPU server. A small model is cheap, but it isn't good at the job. We used the big model to teach small models on three tasks: SQL, financial questions and competition maths. Then we served each student on a single TPU chip. Along the way we found that the recipe has to change when the student is already strong. After that, Fred will show his experiment, which took a different route: teaching models to draw. ~1 min.

Frontier models do the job well, but they are expensive and GPUs are hard to get. Small models are cheap, but they don't know the domain.
Part 1: the problem. ~6 minutes.

Let's start with the hardware. Our teacher, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, is a mixture-of-experts model with 552 billion parameters. One copy of it fills a whole server of eight H200 GPUs, which costs 36 dollars 60 an hour at the cheapest listed rate. GPUs like these are hard to get. Each copy of the model ties up a full server, so serving more users means renting more servers. A single TPU v6e chip costs 2 dollars 70 an hour. The question is whether we can move useful work from the left side of this slide to the right. ~1.5 min.
The problem

Every correct answer has a price

The big model is accurate but expensive
It writes correct SQL for 60.7% of questions, and 1,000 correct answers cost $1.44.
The small model is cheap but often wrong
1,000 correct answers cost only $0.18, but it gets just 30.6% of questions right.
BIRD dev: 1,534 questions on 11 databases never used in training. Cost = $ per hour ÷ (requests per second × 3,600 × accuracy).
We put a price on every correct answer. On the BIRD text-to-SQL benchmark, the teacher gets 60.7 percent of questions right, and a thousand correct answers cost a dollar 44. The untrained small model on one TPU chip costs 18 cents per thousand correct answers, but it gets less than a third of the questions right. We don't want either of these. We want a model in the top-left corner, one that is cheap and accurate. Keep this chart in mind, because we'll come back to it. ~1.5 min.

Why fine-tune for the domain
The untrained 1.7B model gets only 10% of the hardest questions right. Of its wrong answers, 574 return the wrong rows and 462 are not valid SQLite, because the model invents columns or uses another dialect's syntax. Companies see the same problem with their own schemas and SQL dialects.

Here is what "weak in the domain" looks like, with a real BIRD question. The untrained small model escapes the apostrophe the way MySQL does, and SQLite rejects the query. After training, the student uses SQLite's doubled quote and gets the right rows. On the right you can see that the untrained model solves 40 percent of the simple questions and only 10 percent of the hardest ones. 462 of its wrong answers aren't even valid SQL. This is the gap companies care about: their own schemas and their own SQL dialect. ~1.5 min.
The bet
We bet that a big model could teach a small one, and that the small one could run on a single TPU chip.
Before we started, we set two targets: at least 5 points better than the untrained student, and at least 10× cheaper per correct answer than the teacher.
Here was our bet. We use the big model as a teacher, train the small model on its answers, and serve the student on a single TPU chip. Before we started, we wrote down what success would mean: the student had to be at least five points better than before, with a confidence interval above zero, and at least ten times cheaper per correct answer than the teacher. We tested the bet three times, and the third test showed us where it breaks down. ~1 min.

We ran three experiments through the same pipeline. In BIRD, the model writes SQL against a database schema, and the grader runs the query and compares the rows. In FinQA, the model answers a numerical question about a page from an earnings report, which has text and a table, and the grader checks that the number is within one percent. In MATH, the model solves competition problems and puts its answer in a box, and the grader checks for an exact match. Each task is one module in the code, with its own prompt, answer extraction and grader. Everything else is shared: generation, verification, training and serving. Every grader also passes two checks: gold answers score 100 percent and shuffled answers score close to zero. ~1.5 min.
The experiments

One student starts weak, the other starts strong

Qwen3-1.7B starts weak
It is about 30 points behind the teacher on SQL and finance, so it has the most to learn. It is also the cheapest to serve.
Gemma 4 E2B starts strong
It already solves 74.9% of MATH problems. We wanted to know if a teacher still helps.
Untrained student vs teacher, same prompt, greedy decoding; Qwen3-4B already scored 61.7% on BIRD and was not used
We chose each student by how far it was behind the teacher, measured on the same benchmark with the same prompt. Qwen3-1.7B is about 30 points behind on both SQL and finance, so it has a lot to learn. The next size up, Qwen3-4B, already matched the teacher on BIRD, so there was nothing left to teach it. For the third experiment we picked a different kind of student on purpose: Gemma 4 E2B on competition maths. It is already strong, at 74.9 percent, and much closer to the teacher. That let us test whether a teacher still helps a student like that. ~1.5 min.
The experiments

Six decisions shaped the experiments

Tasks
Tasks with automatic graders
A program checks every answer, so one run takes minutes and can show a 2-point change. An agentic benchmark needs hours per run.
Students
A weak student and a strong one
With two students we can see when a teacher helps, and when it doesn't.
Training data
Only verified answers
The grader checks every training answer, because a wrong answer would teach the student the wrong thing.
Format
Short answers
Teacher and student both reason briefly. Without long reasoning traces, the teacher samples 4× faster.
Training
LoRA on one TPU host
LoRA fits on a single TPU v6e-8, and every size is a multiple of 256 to match the chip's matrix unit.
Evaluation
Comparisons within one run
We evaluate every model, a control and a second copy of the baseline together.
Six decisions shaped all three experiments. We picked tasks with automatic graders, so a single run takes a few minutes and can show a two-point change. We used a weak student and a strong one, to see when a teacher helps. We trained only on verified answers. We kept answers short, without long reasoning traces. We trained with LoRA on one TPU host. And we compared models only within the same run, alongside a control and a second copy of the baseline. ~1.5 min.

Here is the whole process on one slide. All three experiments follow it. We measure the gap and the noise. We generate training answers, from the teacher for a weak student or from the student itself for a strong one. We check every answer with the task's grader. We distil with LoRA on one TPU host. We reinforce with GRPO. And we serve each student on one chip and work out the price of each answer. Distillation takes 10 to 27 minutes, and the final students are 4.8 to 17.5 times cheaper per correct answer than the teacher. ~1 min.

What we did
We sampled the teacher 4 times and the student 8 times per question, using training questions only.
Decision
A weak student learns from the teacher's answers. A strong student learns from its own verified answers, which keep its style of reasoning.
What we learned
The training answers should look like what the student will write when it serves. On MATH, the teacher's shorter solutions made a strong student worse.
Step two is choosing who writes the training answers. For a weak student, that's the teacher. We sample it four times per question at temperature 0.7, which gives 25 to 34 thousand answers per task in under 40 minutes. For a strong student, it's the student itself. We sample it eight times per question, and we don't need the teacher at all. We also train on the student's own answers as a control on every task, and on MATH that control won. Both teacher and student write the same short format: brief reasoning, then one answer. ~1.5 min.

What we did
We trained low-rank adapters on all eight chips of one TPU v6e-8 host, merged them into the model and exported a standard checkpoint for serving. Every task used the same settings.
Decision
We used LoRA instead of full fine-tuning because it fits on one host. Every size is a multiple of 256 to match the TPU's matrix unit.
What we learned
Before you trust a result, check that the trainer and the server compute the same model. Keep adapter weights in float32, because in bf16 the optimizer's first update divides zero by zero.
Step four is distillation. We train low-rank adapters on all eight chips of one TPU host, with the same settings on every task. That's 117, 69 and 57 optimizer steps, and 10 to 27 minutes of training. Before trusting any result, we checked that the training model and the serving model agree: they pick the same top token 99.8 percent of the time on Qwen3 and 99.4 percent on Gemma. A full retrain also reproduced a byte-identical checkpoint. One lesson we learned the hard way: adapter weights have to stay in float32. ~1.5 min.
The journey · step 5 of 6

RL makes sometimes-right answers always right

1  Measure
2  Generate
3  Verify
4  Distil
5  Reinforce

What we did
We ran six rounds of GRPO. The student answers each training question 8 times, gets a reward of 1 when the grader accepts an answer, and moves toward the better answers in each group.
Decision
The update learns only from questions with mixed outcomes, because all-right and all-wrong groups give it nothing to compare. A small KL penalty keeps the student close to where it started.
What we learned
On FinQA, the share of questions the student always gets right grew from 43% to 61%, while the share it never gets right barely moved. On held-out data, RL added 4.0 points on BIRD, 5.8 on FinQA and 1.8 on MATH.
Training pool, 8 samples per question at T 1.0, policy that produced each round's samples; BIRD all-wrong = 100 − pass@8, all-right = pass@8 − mixed
Step five is reinforcement learning. GRPO samples the student eight times per training question. An answer gets a reward of one if the grader accepts it, and the update pushes the student toward the better answers in each group. Only questions with mixed outcomes teach it anything. If all eight answers are right, or all eight are wrong, there's nothing to compare. The bars sort every training question, in every round, into three groups. Teal means all eight answers were right, gold means some were right, and gray means all eight were wrong. On FinQA the teal block grows from 43 to 61 percent, and it grows by taking over the gold block. The gray block barely changes. So RL takes answers the student can sometimes reach and makes it give them every time. It doesn't teach the student questions it never solves; that's what distillation is for. On held-out data, RL is worth 4 points on BIRD, 5.8 on FinQA and 1.8 on MATH. ~2 min.

What we did
We served every model with the same flags, one vLLM-TPU copy per chip, and raised the load until errors appeared. Then we priced the best error-free rate.
Decision
We measure the cost of each correct answer, because both accuracy and throughput affect it. Tokens per second would ignore accuracy.
What we learned
Answer length drives throughput. The trained BIRD student is faster than the untrained one, at 15.8 requests per second, but the MATH student's long answers make it slower than the teacher.
Serving cost per 1,000 correct BIRD answers; student at the on-demand TPU v6e rate, teacher at the cheapest listed H200 rate
Step six is serving, and putting a price on each answer. Every model gets the same serving flags and one TPU chip per copy. We raise the load until the server starts returning errors, and we price the best rate it reached without errors. We measure cost per correct answer, because a fast wrong answer is worth nothing. On BIRD, a thousand correct answers from the trained student cost 9 cents. That's half the cost of the untrained model and a sixteenth of the teacher's. Answer length matters a lot here, as we'll see with MATH. ~1 min.

Why did the teacher's answers hurt on MATH? Gemma already reasons well, and it writes long solutions. The teacher writes much shorter ones, about 600 tokens compared with Gemma's thousand. The distilled student copied that style and lost the most on the two hardest levels. It isn't running out of room, either: it hits the length limit less often than before. We also tried teacher corrections, where the teacher sees the student's failed answer and writes a fix, and we train on the fixes. That was even worse, at minus 10.8 points. What we got

Counting cut-off answers as wrong made RL work

Problems per roundwith mixed outcomes
Lesson
About 15% of the student's MATH answers hit the 2,048-token limit before giving an answer, and our original GRPO threw them away. Once we scored them as wrong, the update had twice as many problems to learn from, and GRPO added 1.82 points [+1.00, +2.66] instead of 0.84.
MATH test, 5,000 problems, same-run evaluation; 6 GRPO rounds each; cut-off answers keep reward 0 and no end-of-turn token in the loss
We had to make one more change before RL worked for the strong student. Gemma's most common failure is running out of room: about 15 percent of its answers hit the 2,048-token limit before giving a final answer. Our original GRPO threw those answers away, so the student's main failure never got a negative reward. We kept them, gave them a reward of zero, and trained only on the tokens the student actually wrote. That doubled the number of problems with mixed outcomes in each round, from about 600 to 1,300, and GRPO then added 1.82 points instead of 0.84. ~1.5 min.

How does this compare with other work? At the PyTorch Conference, Meta presented TorchTitan RL for long-horizon agents. They trained a Qwen3.5 9B model with RL on about a thousand cleaned terminal tasks, using eight B300s split into trainer, rollout and eval workers that run fully asynchronously. Their problem and scale are different. They go straight to RL on multi-turn agents, while we distil first and then run RL on single-turn answers. But the lessons are similar. Both projects cleaned the data first: only 42 percent of their tasks were clean, and 40 percent of our teacher's answers were wrong. Both checked the numerics: a compiled kernel gave them a 2.5x log-prob mismatch, and we check that our trainer and server agree. And both had to reward failures carefully. They treat a dead sandbox, a missing submission and the context limit differently, and our MATH fix, counting cut-off answers as wrong, is the same idea. ~2 min.

How to choose the recipe
Train on the teacher's answers and on the student's own answers side by side. If the teacher's answers win, use KD + GRPO. If the student's own answers win, use self-distillation + GRPO, which needs no teacher time.
Code, settings and commands: github.com/jawadaminGOOG/tpu-distil (README recipe, docs/RECIPE.md)
Here's the recipe, so you can try it on your own task. Measure the gap and the noise with one prompt. Generate two training sets, one from the teacher and one from the student itself. Keep only the answers your grader accepts, and check for leaks. Distil both sets with the same LoRA settings and keep the better one. That one comparison tells you whether your student is weak or strong. Then run GRPO with the grader as the reward, and count cut-off answers as wrong. Finally, serve on single chips and work out the price of a correct answer. The code you write is one task module. It loads the questions, builds a versioned prompt, extracts the answer and grades it, with gold and shuffled checks. We added FinQA and MATH exactly this way. ~2 min.

A28Appendix
Supports slide 29 · Why we trust these results

What these results don't show

One training seed
We trained each model once, so the noise floor measures serving noise, not training-seed variance.
Student and task changed together
The student changed along with the task, so "already strong" is our best explanation, not a proven cause.
Public benchmarks
The teacher and students may have seen BIRD, FinQA or MATH during pre-training. This applies to every model.
The FinQA grader is lenient
It accepts a 1% tolerance and percentage forms, so our scores aren't comparable to published FinQA results.
We missed the MATH targets
We required KD to gain 5 points (it lost 2.3) and each GRPO stage to gain 2 (the best gained 1.82). A 7-hour outage cut one round to 3,367 of 6,262 problems.
The cost gap depends on deployment
A faster teacher deployment would shrink the cost advantage (see A2).

A32Appendix
Supports slide 34 · Meta's TorchTitan RL work found similar lessons

A closer look at TorchTitan RL

Sampling never pauses
Rollouts run continuously, new weights are swapped in while they run, and the buffer takes any 32 finished groups in any order.
The KV cache survives updates
They keep it across weight updates with no accuracy loss. KV headroom limits how many rollouts run in parallel.
The agent runs outside the sandbox
The policy controls a sandbox but doesn't run inside it.
Each failure gets its own reward
A dead sandbox gets no penalty, a missing submission gets a penalty, and hitting the context limit gets a nudge.
They cleaned the tasks
Only 42% of TMAX tasks and 61% of CalibForge tasks were clean before repair.
DPPO with per-prompt averaging
They use DPPO instead of GRPO or PPO, and average the loss per prompt instead of per token.
Speaker's notes of "Training long-horizon agents with TorchTitan RL", PyTorch Conference 2026
